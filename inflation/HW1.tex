\begin{question}
If the price of a gaming console goes from $\$500$ to $\$512$ in one month, we might say the rate of change is $\answer{12}$ dollars per month.  But a $\$12$ price increase would outrageous for a $\$15$ take-out-meal and insignificant for a $\$30,000$ new car. Thus, price changes are more sensibly conceived, calculated, and reported as \textbf{percent increases or decreases}.  

\begin{itemize}
\item The price increase for the gaming console would be $\answer{2.4}$ percent.  
\item A comparable price increase for the take-out-meal would be $\answer{0.36}$ dollars. 
\item A comparable price increase for the new car would be $\answer{720}$ dollars. 
\end{itemize}
\begin{hint}
We are looking for price \textbf{increase}, not the new price.  Comparable price increases would be the same percentage of the original price.  
\end{hint}
\end{question}

To begin our discussion of inflation data reported by the federal government, here is some helpful background: 
\begin{itemize}
\item The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) tracks the prices of many goods and services in the U.S. economy.  The data is compiled monthly to compute the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which is a weighted average of a representative ``market basket'' of goods and services purchased by typical U.S. households.  
% The BLS tracks prices of tens-of-thousands of items in about 200 categories, arranged into eight major groups (e.g., apparel, transportation). 
\item The BLS releases different versions of the CPI for different regions of the country and different population groups, but the most commonly reported measure is the CPI for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U) which covers over 90 percent of the total population.  
\end{itemize}

\begin{question}
From May to June, 2022, the CPI increased from 291.298 to 294.957.  
\begin{enumerate}
\item What was the month-over-month inflation rate to the nearest hundredth of a percent?   $\answer[tolerance=0.04]{1.26}$ percent.  

Inflation can be calculated over any number of months, but to compare inflation over different periods of time, it helps to compare annualized rates, just as loans and certificates of deposit are usually described with an ``annual percentage rate'' (APR). 

\item If this monthly rate continued for 12 months, what would be the annual inflation rate to the nearest tenth of a percent?  $\answer[tolerance=0.06]{16.2}$ percent. 

This is a very high rate of inflation.  

\item In the following month, July, 2022, the CPI was 294.913, which indicates a very small month-to-month \textbf{decrease} in prices, and a month-over-month rate of inflation near $\answer{0}$ percent.  These large differences between consecutive months demonstrate that month-over-month measures can be ``noisy'' and misleading.  The common first approach for overcoming potentially  noisy data is to look at inflation over a full \wordChoice{\choice{month}\choice[correct]{year}\choice{decade}}, as illustrated in the next problem.   
\end{enumerate}

\begin{question}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Prices of some goods and services (such as gasoline and some foods) are ``volatile,'' meaning they fluctuate dramatically within a month or even within a day.  Thus, the BLS also reports another measure, called \textbf{Core CPI}, which removes food and energy prices from the calculation, thereby helping economists and policy makers see a more stable estimate of ``underlying'' inflation.
\item The Federal Reserve System (the Fed) uses $2\%$ (annual) \textbf{core} inflation as a ``target'' to guide its policy decisions.    
\item Prices of other goods and services (such as wages and rent) are described as ``sticky,'' meaning they respond slowly to changing economic conditions.  
\end{enumerate}

When inflation is changing fast, these year-over-year measures can be misleading because the comparison data is more than 12 months old.  On the other hand, as we have already seen, month-over-month measures can be ``noisy'' and thus also misleading.  As a compromise between these approaches, economists sometimes attend to inflation over 3 or 6 months, and then annualize those measures.  

With a modest interest rate, debt can be manageable.  With slight inflation, debt becomes more manageable over time.  For example, when inflation is $2\%$, the ``real'' cost of a $5\%$ mortgage is only $\answer{3}$ percent.  Many homeowners stretch to afford their first mortgage.  But with a little inflation, alongside raises and promotions at work, homeowners often find their mortgages become more affordable after $5$ or $10$ years.  

%As we have seen above, slight month-over-month deflation is not uncommon due to many factors, including the following: 
%
%(1) Prices of most goods and services change only slightly, if at all. 
%(2) Prices of other goods (e.g., food and energy) are quite volatile.  
%(3) The process involves inherent measurement error.  

Month-over-month deflation is not uncommon, but year-over-year deflation is rare. Significant or prolonged deflation is undesirable, as falling prices are a sign of a weak economy.  Consumers delay purchases in hopes of getting a lower price in the future.  Companies slow production in response to lower demand, and eventually resort to layoffs or wage reductions, aiming to maintain profitability.  The real value of consumer and corporate debt increases, making it harder to pay off.  Unemployment rises, which reduces demand, which further reduces prices.
